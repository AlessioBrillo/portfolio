## Context

I fly ultralight aircraft under the Italian **VDS licence** (Volo da Diporto e
Sportivo). The licence is earned, not bought: a written theory syllabus —
meteorology, air law, navigation, flight mechanics — plus a practical syllabus
of solo circuits, cross-country legs and a final check-ride. It grants
privileges, and it grants them with limits. Both halves matter.

The site's narrative thread is the sky, and this is the study of it: what the
licence actually took, and what flying an ultralight teaches that transfers
straight back to engineering.

## Problem

Flying an ultralight in Italy is a discipline problem disguised as a hobby.
Three things make it genuinely hard:

1. **Weather is the real opponent.** Ultralights are light, slow and have no
   IFR capability — the same sun that makes a perfect afternoon can build
   into a wind that grounds the aircraft by noon. Reading the sky honestly,
   all day, every flight, is the difference between a pleasant circuit and
   a decision I should not have made.
2. **The licence is a ceiling, not a ticket.** VDS privileges are conditional:
   class of aircraft, daylight hours, visibility minima, no cloud penetration.
   The professional skill is *using* the freedom inside the limits — and
   stopping at them without resentment.
3. **The gap between theory and air.** Ground school teaches that a
   crosswind component must be computed; the aircraft teaches that it must be
   *felt* — on the ailerons, the rudder, the seat of the pants. The gap
   between knowing and doing is where every incident lives.

## Approach

I treated the licence like a project with a syllabus and a test plan, not a
series of lessons:

### Phase 1 — Theory, closed loops

Meteorology first. Every lesson ended with a question I could not answer, and
every unanswered question became the homework of the next. Air law was learned
as rules I would *apply* on a real map, not paragraphs to recite: each
regulation was mapped to the manoeuvre it exists to protect.

### Phase 2 — Circuits, then cross-country

The pattern work came first — takeoff, downwind, base, final, flare, roll-out —
repeated until the aircraft stopped being a machine and became an extension of
the seat. Only then did the syllabus open into navigation: planning a
cross-country, reading a chart, talking to the radio, finding the field.

### Phase 3 — The check-ride as a review, not a verdict

The final check-ride was scheduled late enough that it measured a *habit*, not
a crammed week. My standard for "ready" was simple: I should be able to
execute the entire syllabus on a bad day, with a wind I did not choose, and
explain every decision in the debrief.

> The same rule I apply to code: if it only works when everything goes right,
> it does not work. It works when it survives the conditions you did not pick.

## What I built

The artefacts of the licence are not files in a repo, but they are artefacts
nonetheless:

- **A personal operations manual** — my own minima, written down: winds,
  ceilings, fuel margins, personal currency rules. Stricter than the legal
  floor, on purpose, because the legal floor is a floor, not a target.
- **A debrief log** — every flight, one line per landing: what the wind did,
  what I corrected, what I would redo. The log is the training data.
- **A go/no-go ritual** — a fixed pre-flight sequence, executed the same way
  every time, so that deciding *not* to fly becomes a routine decision and not
  an emotional one.

## Result / Reflection

The licence is a credential; the transferable parts are the habits around it:

1. **Decision hygiene beats skill.** The single most important flying skill is
   the discipline of saying no — to a gusty crosswind, to a cloud deck
   building, to "one more circuit". Engineering is the same: the best pull
   requests are often the ones that never get opened, and the best production
   incidents are the ones a careful operator prevented.
2. **Minima you write down are real.** An unwritten personal minimum is a
   preference; a written one is a contract with yourself. In code, the
   equivalent is a test that encodes the invariant *before* the pressure
   arrives.
3. **Feeling the aircraft is calibration.** Instruments tell you what is
   happening; the seat tells you what is about to happen. The same is true of
   a running system — metrics report the past, and the operator's trained
   sense for what is *off* is what buys the minutes before the alert fires.

Would I do it differently? I would log more cross-countries, earlier. The
circuits built the foundation, but the legs are where the licence became a
competence — the same way the foundation of this site's engineering was built
in small, well-tested pieces, and the real learning came from shipping it end
to end.
