## Context

The VDS licence is the narrative thread of this site — and the companion study
(`/sky/vds-licence`) tells the discipline side of that story: how the licence
was earned as an exercise in decision hygiene. This study is the other half of
the same coin. Every flight manual hands you numbers: stall speed, best glide,
minimum power. I wanted to stop _quoting_ them and start _deriving_ them — the
same instinct that made me train a tokenizer instead of borrowing one: no
conclusion on trust, every number on first principles.

The aircraft regime matters. Ultralight aerodynamics do not follow airliner
intuition: wing loadings around 30–45 kg/m² against roughly 600 kg/m² for a
narrow-body jet, Reynolds numbers an order of magnitude lower, and a weather
vulnerability that turns a 10-knot gust into a margin problem. The physics is
not harder — it is just different, and the difference is the point.

## Problem

Three knots sit between a pilot and the numbers in the manual:

1. **The manual gives results, not reasons.** `Vy`, `Vx`, best-glide speed and
   the stall figure are quoted as facts. Quoted facts survive weather and
   pressure altitude poorly — they are correct _at one configuration and one
   weight_, and the pilot has to know which direction they move when either
   changes.
2. **Low wing loading is a double-edged safety margin.** It buys a low stall
   speed — the slowest, safest-feeling number in the whole machine — but it
   also means the aircraft _is_ a wind gauge: gust response scales with
   wing loading, and the margin that feels like forgiveness is the same margin
   a gust can eat in a second.
3. **The landing flare is the most physics-dense second in the flight** and it
   hides a subtlety: ground effect. Within about one wingspan of the surface,
   induced drag collapses and the wing "floats". Flare too early and the float
   eats the runway; flare too late and the sink is yours.

## Approach

Four stages, each ending in a number I can defend in the cockpit.

### Stage 1 — Stall speed from the lift equation

The lift equation `L = ½ρV²SC_L` sets the floor: at the stall, lift just
balances weight at `C_L,max`, so

```text
V_stall = sqrt(2W / (ρ S C_L,max))
```

Everything that matters is visible in that one root: weight scales the stall
speed as its square root, and wing loading `W/S` is the master dial of the
whole flight envelope. With the manual's `C_L,max` and my aircraft's wing
loading (_fill in from the POH: wing area, MTOW, C_L,max_), the book stall
figure drops out of the equation instead of being looked up. The derivation
then answers the question the manual cannot: _which way does the stall speed
move at my actual take-off weight and at altitude?_ — down with a lighter
aircraft, up with thinner air.

### Stage 2 — The drag polar and the glide that has no engine

Total drag splits into parasite and induced:

`D = D_p + D_i`, with `D_i = W² / (½ρV²πb²e)`

Parasite drag grows with `V²`; induced drag — the price of making lift — falls
as `1/V²`. Their sum has a minimum, and that minimum _is_ the best glide: at
`L/D_max` the aircraft travels farthest per metre of altitude, and the glide
ratio equals the lift-to-drag ratio. Ultralights sit around 8–12:1, an
airliner around 18:1 — the glide is short, so the engine-out plan is made
before take-off, not after the silence. (My aircraft's measured figure from
the POH glide data: fill in.)

### Stage 3 — Ground effect and the flare that floats

Induced drag is the cost of the downwash the wing leaves behind it. Near the
ground — within roughly one wingspan — the surface blocks that downwash and
the induced drag drops sharply: the same wing now needs less angle of attack
for the same lift, and the aircraft _floats_ through the round-out. This is
why the flare is a sight picture, not a manoeuvre: pull the attitude too early
and ground effect converts the error into runway eaten at a decreasing rate;
too late and the touchdown is yours at the sink rate of the last second. The
physics is the same one that makes a low pass feel "fast" and a flare feel
"suspended".

### Stage 4 — The decision gate: where physics becomes discipline

The last derivation is the go/no-go. Approach speed carries a gust factor by
design — the convention is half the gust on top of the base figure — and the
gust factor is itself a physics statement: a gusty day widens the speed band
between stall and the margins the pilot actually wants. On an aircraft whose
stall sits at ultralight speeds, a 15-knot gust front is not an annoyance, it
is a computed margin reduction. The discipline study tells the story of the
licence; this study is the spreadsheet behind the weather call. A concrete

## What I built

Not a system — deliberately. The deliverable is a set of derived numbers: a
one-page sheet that starts from the lift and drag equations and ends at the
figures I brief before every flight — stall at today's weight, best glide,
the gust-adjusted approach speed. The derivations live next to the VDS
notebook, and the sheet is re-derived whenever the aircraft or the weight
envelope changes, so it cannot quietly go stale the way a quoted figure can.

The meta-result is the one that generalizes beyond the cockpit:

1. **Derived numbers stick; quoted numbers drift.** The formula gives you the
   _direction_ of every change before the aircraft gives you the feeling.
2. **The ultralight regime punishes borrowed intuition.** Airliner instincts —
   long float, generous margins, room to think — are luxuries of a wing
   loading ten times higher; the physics explains exactly why.
3. **The decision gate is where the two studies meet.** The licence study is
   the discipline; this study is the evidence behind it. Neither is complete
   without the other, and the site is the only place both are published.

Would I do it differently? The sheet could grow into a small interactive
glide/performance calculator — but the point was never the tool; it was to
own the numbers that decide the flight, and to be able to say _why_ in the
few seconds a weather decision allows.
